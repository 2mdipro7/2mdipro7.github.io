Product & UX analytics · Public dataset analysis

Find where browsing stops becoming a purchase

The largest measurable loss in this public electronics store dataset happened before shoppers added to cart. I analysed the event journey to separate that finding from checkout assumptions and give a product team specific questions to investigate.

My role
Analyst, session preparation and funnel interpretation
Work
Event cleaning, session analysis, segment comparisons and propensity modelling
Project context
Historical public dataset analysis, no client store changes

01 / Question

Where does browsing stop becoming intent?

A low purchase rate can tempt a team to start with checkout. I examined a historical electronics store clickstream to find which stage actually lost the most sessions before choosing a UX review priority.

The dataset records views, cart events and purchases. Those events provide a useful journey, but they do not record the reason a person leaves or a separate checkout step.

02 / My work

Define a funnel the events can support.

I cleaned 884,964 events and grouped them into 490,398 sessions. I then compared journeys by category, product and behavioural segment, keeping the stage definitions consistent across those views.

Cart behaviour was the measurable intent stage. The generic preparation pipeline reused a cart flag as a checkout intent proxy, so I separated that technical label from the interpretation: no distinct checkout event was available.

This decision prevented a cart to purchase gap from being presented as a diagnosis of a particular checkout screen.

03 / Finding

Investigate the move from product detail to cart.

Of 488,360 sessions reaching product detail, 41,270 reached cart and 24,344 reached purchase. Product detail to cart conversion was 8.45%; among cart sessions, 58.99% reached purchase.

The largest measured loss was before cart. I used category comparisons to identify where product information, comparison and navigation deserved closer review. A propensity model also explored high intent non buyers as a queue for investigation.

The model and segment patterns are ways to prioritise questions. They do not identify an individual’s motive or show that a proposed interface change will increase purchases.

04 / Next decision

Turn the pattern into a testable UX question.

The result was a clearer starting point for product investigation: inspect the information and choices people encounter before adding an item, rather than assume checkout was the main bottleneck.

A useful next check is whether important product information is missing or difficult to compare. Another is whether records in an unknown category reflect a taxonomy problem. Observation and a controlled change would be needed to evaluate either explanation.

This work analysed a public historical dataset. It did not redesign a live client store or deliver a measured conversion lift.
